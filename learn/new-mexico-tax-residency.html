New Mexico's 185-Day Residency Rule

Being physically present in New Mexico for 185 days or more in a tax year makes you a New Mexico resident, regardless of domicile. Only full 24-hour periods count. Here is how the count works.

Last verified: November 2026

In short: New Mexico treats you as a resident if you are physically present in the state for 185 days or more during the tax year, regardless of your domicile and with no home condition. A day counts only if it is a full 24-hour period in New Mexico: partial days, however many, do not count.

The rule

New Mexico residents are people domiciled in New Mexico, and people physically present in the state for 185 days or more during the tax year, regardless of domicile. Two details set the presence test apart:

  • A day is 24 hours. New Mexico's regulations define a day as any consecutive 24-hour period. The Taxation and Revenue Department's instructions say to count each day you were here for 24 hours: someone who works in New Mexico on 185 partial days is not a resident.
  • No home is needed. Students, vacationers and people on temporary work assignments who plan to return home still become residents at 185 days.

How to count it

  1. Count the full 24-hour periods you spent in New Mexico. Day trips and short visits that last less than 24 hours do not count.
  2. Add the periods across the tax year, 1 January to 31 December for most people. They do not need to be consecutive.
  3. If the total reaches 185, New Mexico treats you as a resident for that year.

Example. You spend half the year in Santa Fe, arriving on 10 March at 9:00 and leaving on 10 September at 9:00. That stay touches 185 calendar days, but it holds 184 full 24-hour periods.

New Mexico counts the 24-hour periods, so you have 184 days and are not a resident on presence alone. Staying until 11 September at 9:00 would make it 185.

Nights are an estimate of full 24-hour periods. Arrival and departure times, and clock changes, can make the two counts differ. Use exact times to check a count close to 185.

Beyond the day count

Two groups are treated differently. If you moved to New Mexico during the year intending to make it your permanent home and are still there on 31 December, you are a first-year resident whatever your day count. A military servicemember temporarily assigned to New Mexico who has established legal residence for withholding purposes elsewhere is not a resident.

FAQ

Do partial days count toward New Mexico's 185 days?

No. A day is any consecutive 24-hour period. Time in the state that does not add up to 24 hours, such as a commute or a day trip, does not count.

Do you need a home in New Mexico for the 185-day rule to apply?

No. Physical presence for 185 days or more makes you a resident regardless of domicile or housing.

Do the 185 days have to be consecutive?

No. The 24-hour periods are added across the whole tax year.

Official sources

NMSA 1978 § 7-2-2(S), with the definition of a day in 3.3.1.7 NMAC, and the New Mexico Taxation and Revenue Department's PIT-1 instructions, which explain the 24-hour count.
