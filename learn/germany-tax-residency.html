Germany Tax Residency: The Six-Month Rule and Trips Abroad

Germany has both a home-based test and a habitual-abode rule. Its six-month rule is not equivalent to adding 183 physical-presence days in a calendar year. Check your own dates in the calculator below.

A home is one route

Section 8 of the Fiscal Code describes a residence where someone maintains a dwelling in circumstances indicating that it will be retained and used. An annual presence total cannot establish whether those circumstances apply.

The six-month rule is not a yearly allowance

Section 9 treats a continuous stay exceeding six months as habitual abode from its beginning and disregards short interruptions. It also has an exception for certain exclusively private visits lasting no more than a year. Both the nature of the stay and the continuity matter.

Why a rolling counter is insufficient

A brief trip abroad may reduce the physical days shown in a log without interrupting the legal period. The period can also cross New Year. Neither a fresh annual total nor a per-entry counter that restarts at every border crossing can determine the result.

What to record

Keep travel dates, the reasons for interruptions and evidence about accommodation. A person who spends five months in Germany, makes a short trip abroad and returns should not assume the legal clock restarted. An annual count is a useful summary of presence, but there is no universal safe number to display for all situations.

Why a seven-day trip can mislead a counter

Illustrative physical record: 1 January to 31 March 2026 is 90 days in Germany. After a trip abroad from 1 to 7 April, a second German stay from 8 April to 8 July contributes 92 days. The recorded physical total is 182.

A naive 183-day meter would suggest the number is still below its line. But the legal question includes whether the short interruption is disregarded within a continuous stay exceeding six months. The physical total alone cannot resolve that, and the statutory private-visit exception must also be considered where relevant.

Record interruptions without assuming they reset anything

Keep the dates, purpose and circumstances of trips abroad, together with evidence about the accommodation retained in Germany. Do not erase the interruption from your physical-presence log: the log should stay factual. Any legal treatment of the interruption is a separate assessment.

This distinction prevents two opposite mistakes: treating every departure as a complete reset, or counting every day abroad as if you were physically in Germany. The travel record should not be altered to manufacture either conclusion.

A useful question for an adviser

Instead of asking “Do I have one day left before 183?”, present the two stays and ask whether they form one habitual-abode period, whether the retained accommodation establishes a residence, and whether a statutory exception applies. That makes the uncertainty specific and lets the dates support the analysis.

FAQ

Is Germany’s rule simply 183 days per year?

No. Home and habitual abode are separate concepts, and the statutory duration rule uses a continuous period exceeding six months.

Does a short trip abroad reset the period?

Not necessarily. Section 9 explicitly disregards short interruptions.

Can I use an annual day record?

Yes, as a record of physical presence. It does not assess the home test, continuity or the statutory exceptions.

Official sources

Fiscal Code section 8, residence; Fiscal Code section 9, habitual abode.
