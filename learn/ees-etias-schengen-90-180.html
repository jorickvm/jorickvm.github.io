EES and ETIAS: What They Mean for Your Schengen Days

Two EU border systems, two different jobs, and one thing neither of them changes: the 90/180 allowance itself.

Last verified: August 2026

In short: EES is a border system that has replaced passport stamping with a digital record of your entries and exits. It has been fully operational since 10 April 2026, costs nothing, and requires no application. ETIAS is a travel authorisation that visa-exempt travelers will apply for online before departure; it is expected in the last quarter of 2026. Neither changes the short-stay rule. You still get 90 days in any rolling 180-day period across the Schengen area. What changed is that the record of how you used them is now automatic.

Two Systems, Two Different Jobs

They are constantly confused because both are new, both are European, and both have four-letter acronyms. But they answer different questions at different moments.

EES ETIAS
What it isA border record of entries and exits.A travel authorisation obtained in advance.
When it happensAt the border, on arrival and departure.Online, before you travel.

Do you apply?No. It happens to you at the booth.Yes, before booking or boarding.

The short version: EES is something that is recorded about you. ETIAS is something you have to do. If you are trying to work out whether you need to take an action before a trip, that is the ETIAS question, and today the answer is still not yet.

What EES Actually Records

EES began a phased rollout on 12 October 2025 and has been fully operational at the external borders of the Schengen countries since 10 April 2026. For non-EU nationals on short stays it replaces the passport stamp with a digital file that holds your travel document data, a facial image, fingerprints, and the date and place of every entry and exit.

Two consequences matter for day counting.

  • Your dates are no longer only in your passport. The stamp was a physical artefact that could be faint, missing, or unreadable, and that an officer had to find and add up under time pressure. The record is now queryable.
  • Overstay detection is automatic. The system is designed to flag travelers who have exceeded their authorised stay, rather than relying on someone noticing at the booth.

There is an optional convenience: travelers can pre-register some of their details in the official Travel to Europe mobile app within 72 hours before arrival, which is intended to speed up the check at the border. It is not an authorisation and it does not replace the border process.

Not everyone is registered. EES does not apply to nationals of Andorra, Monaco, San Marino, and Vatican City, nor to non-EU nationals holding a long-stay visa or a residence permit issued by a country operating EES, nor to non-EU family members of EU or EEA citizens who hold a valid residence card. If you hold a residence permit, you are outside the short-stay system this article describes, and you should be counting against your permit's own conditions instead.

What ETIAS Is, and When It Starts

ETIAS is an online travel authorisation for travelers from visa-exempt countries, covering 30 European countries. You apply before departure, approval is normally required before boarding, and most applications are decided within minutes. An approved authorisation is valid for 3 years, or until your passport expires, whichever comes first.

It is not in force yet. The Commission expects operations to start in the last quarter of 2026 and has said the exact date will be announced several months beforehand. Two cushions follow the start:

  1. A transitional period of at least 6 months. You are expected to apply, but travelers without an authorisation will not be refused entry if they meet the other entry conditions.
  2. A grace period of at least 6 months after that, during which first-time travelers since the end of the transitional period may still be admitted exceptionally, again provided they meet the other conditions.

Treat any specific launch date, fee, or eligibility list you read elsewhere as provisional until the Commission confirms it. This is exactly the kind of detail that moves.

What Neither of Them Changes

This is the part worth being unambiguous about, because the arrival of two border systems has convinced a lot of people that the underlying allowance must have changed too.

It has not. The short-stay rule is still 90 days in any rolling 180-day period, still pooled across the whole Schengen area rather than counted per country, and the day of entry and the day of exit both still count. For the mechanics, see What Does 90 Days in Any 180-Day Period Mean? and the rolling window walkthrough.

What changed is enforcement quality. Under stamping, a miscount often survived because nobody reconstructed it. Under EES, the authoritative version of your movements is already assembled before anyone asks you about it.

Where That Leaves Your Own Record

It is tempting to conclude that if the system now counts for you, you no longer need to. That gets the direction of the change backwards.

  • You cannot query it the way a border officer can. Knowing your position before you book is still your problem, and the answer depends on the date you plan to enter.
  • Ground travel and non-Schengen legs still shape the count. Your own record is what ties a whole itinerary together.
  • An automatic record raises the cost of being wrong. A guess that disagrees with the file is now likelier to be caught, not less.
  • Other rules still need your dates. Tax-residence tests, settlement absences, and visa applications ask for the same travel history in different shapes, and none of them read the EES file.

Practical Caution and Official Boundary

This page explains what the two systems are and how they relate to short-stay counting. It is not a statement of your legal position, and it does not tell you whether you personally need an ETIAS. For the current status and the official detail, use the European Commission's Entry/Exit System overview and the EU's ETIAS information for travelers, together with the short-stay calculator for the 90/180 arithmetic itself. Where an entry decision matters, the border authority decides, not an article.

FAQ

Does EES change the 90 days in 180 rule?

No. EES records your entry and exit dates digitally instead of stamping your passport, and it detects overstays automatically, but the allowance is unchanged: 90 days in any rolling 180-day period across the Schengen area, with the day of entry and the day of exit both counting.

Do I need an ETIAS right now?

Not yet. ETIAS is expected to start in the last quarter of 2026, and a transitional period of at least 6 months follows, during which travelers without one will not be refused entry if they meet the other conditions. The Commission has said it will announce the exact start date several months in advance.

Will my passport still be stamped?

For short stays covered by EES, the digital record replaces the stamp. Some travelers are outside EES entirely, including holders of long-stay visas and residence permits issued by a country operating the system, and nationals of Andorra, Monaco, San Marino, and Vatican City.
