Does Your Day Count Reset on 1 January? Calendar Year vs Rolling Windows

Whether your days start again at New Year depends on the window the rule uses. There are four kinds, and they give different answers for the same trips.

Last verified: November 2026

Only a calendar-year rule resets on 1 January. A rolling window never resets: each day drops out exactly one window-length after you spent it. A tax year resets on its own date, such as 6 April in the UK or 1 July in Australia. A per-entry limit restarts when you enter again. Find the window first, then count.

The four windows

Calendar year
1 January to 31 December. The count goes back to zero at New Year, even in the middle of a trip.
Tax or fiscal year
A fixed year with its own start date. It resets on that date, not on 1 January.
Rolling window
The last 180 days, 12 months or 5 years, counted back from the day you check. It moves forward one day at a time and never resets.
Per entry
One visit, counted from the day you arrive. It restarts with a new entry, if the country lets you back in.

Which window does your rule use?

The number in a rule's name says how many days. It does not say which window. These are the rules most travelers run into.

Schengen short stay
Rolling. 90 days in any 180 days. Nothing resets in January.
Türkiye visa-free stay
Rolling. 90 days in any 180 days.
US Visa Waiver Program
Per entry. Up to 90 days from each admission.
Thailand tax residency
Calendar year. 180 days between 1 January and 31 December.
US Substantial Presence Test
Calendar year, with a weighted look back over the two years before it.
UK Statutory Residence Test
Tax year. 6 April to 5 April.
Australia 183-day test
Income year. 1 July to 30 June.
Canada citizenship
Rolling. 1,095 days in the 5 years before the day you apply.

One trip, four answers

Take a stay from 1 December 2026 to 31 January 2027, counting the arrival and departure days. That is 62 days: 31 in December and 31 in January.

Calendar year
31 days count toward 2026 and 31 toward 2027. On 1 January the 2027 count starts at 1.
Rolling 180 days
On 31 January all 62 days are inside the window. The December days stay in it until late May and June.
Tax year from 1 July
All 62 days fall in the year from 1 July 2026 to 30 June 2027.
Per entry
62 days, because you entered once.

The same trip is 31 days under one rule and 62 under another. Leaving a country, or waiting for New Year, changes the count only under the rules that work that way.

How a rolling window gives days back

Under a rolling rule, a day stops counting when it falls out of the back of the window. With a 180-day window, a day you spent on 5 August is last counted on 31 January and is gone on 1 February. Days come back one at a time, in the order you spent them, so a long stay is returned slowly and a few short trips are returned in pieces. When you get Schengen days back works through both cases.

This is also why checking only your arrival date is not enough. A planned stay has to fit the window on every day of the trip, including the last.

Three mistakes that cost days

  • Treating 180 days as six months, or 12 months as 365 days. Six calendar months run from 181 to 184 days, and a 12-month period with a leap day has 366. Use the unit the rule uses.
  • Assuming a new year or a new passport stamp clears the count. It does so only for calendar-year and per-entry rules.
  • Counting the right window with the wrong days. Some rules count any part of a day, others count nights or full days. See what counts as a day.

FAQ

Does the Schengen 90/180 count reset on 1 January?

No. It is a rolling window. Days from last year keep counting until each one is more than 180 days old.

Are 12 months always 365 days?

No. A 12-month period that includes 29 February has 366 days. If the rule says months, count months.

Can one trip count in two years?

Yes. Under a calendar-year or tax-year rule, the days on each side of the boundary go to different years. A rolling window can hold the whole trip at once.

How do I find out which window applies to me?

Read the rule's own wording for the period: "in a calendar year", "in any 180-day period", "in the tax year", or "per stay". The guides linked above name the window and the official source for each rule.
