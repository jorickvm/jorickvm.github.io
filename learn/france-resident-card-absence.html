France's Ten-Year Resident Card: The Three-Year Absence Rule

The ordinary ten-year resident card can become invalid after more than three consecutive years abroad. That absence rule is separate from the expiry date printed on the card and from renewal requirements.

Last verified: September 2026

In short: For the ordinary French resident card covered here, residence abroad for more than three consecutive years can make the card invalid. The period concerns a continuous absence, not the sum of separate holidays. The legal period is measured in calendar years, so a fixed total of days is not an exact substitute.

Card covered
The ordinary ten-year carte de résident
Absence condition
More than three consecutive years abroad
Relevant territory
France; do not substitute the whole Schengen area
Separate questions
The card's printed expiry and its renewal requirements
Legal basis
CESEDA, Article L411-5; Service Public ten-year resident-card guidance

The rule

A card described as valid for ten years does not mean its holder can spend that entire period living abroad. The absence provision concerns the continuing validity of the resident card, while its printed expiry tells you when the document's stated term ends.

  • Continuity matters. The rule is written around consecutive years abroad. Adding unrelated trips over the life of the card does not reproduce that test.
  • The territory matters. This guide addresses absence from France for the ordinary card. Spending time in another European country is not automatically the same as being back in France.
  • The card category matters. Article L411-5 contains separate wording for long-term resident–EU cards. Special nationality provisions and other residence documents need to be checked separately.

Service Public states the ordinary card's three-year absence rule directly. Keep that specific scope when reading advice about residence cards more generally: a page discussing a different document can be accurate without answering the question about the card you hold.

How to count it

Begin with the date you left and the calendar anniversary three years later. A separate day total can document the trip, but one fixed number cannot express every three-year calendar interval.

  1. Identify the exact resident-card category and confirm that this absence rule applies to it.
  2. Record the departure date and the actual return date, or keep the absence open while still abroad.
  3. Calculate the three-year calendar reference date. Compare it with the trip dates rather than only with a total of 1095.
  4. Use the recorded-day total as a planning aid and check the card's validity and renewal requirements separately.

Example. Leave France on 1 March 2023 and return on 1 March 2026. The travel dates are three calendar years apart. They are 1096 days apart because the interval includes 29 February 2024. With both travel dates counted as French presence for this illustration, the full days away total 1095.

Now compare 1 March 2024 to 1 March 2027. Those dates are also three calendar years apart, but there is no leap day between them. The elapsed interval is 1095 days and there are 1094 complete absent days.

These day totals fall at different positions relative to the calendar anniversary. Set your return plan using the actual calendar dates rather than a fixed day allowance. These examples explain the arithmetic, not a decision about a particular card's validity.

Beyond the day count

A return date and a residence history are related but not interchangeable. A travel record shows physical stays. A short visit does not by itself establish all the residence facts needed for the card or its renewal.

Renewal also has its own requirements, including the rules on habitual residence described by Service Public. Another nationality-based arrangement, a long-term resident–EU card or a situation involving an overseas territory may need a different analysis. Use the official page for the exact document and circumstances rather than applying one absence period to every French residence status.

FAQ

Does a ten-year card let me live abroad for ten years?

No. The absence rule can affect the card before its printed expiry. For the ordinary card discussed here, the relevant period is more than three consecutive years abroad.

Are three years always 1095 days?

No. A three-year interval can include a leap day. Counting only complete days between departure and return is also a different measurement from comparing the calendar anniversaries.

Does this apply unchanged to a long-term resident–EU card?

Do not assume so. The law contains separate wording for that card. Check the exact document and the territory relevant to its rule instead of substituting the ordinary-card rule.

Official sources

France's Service Public guide to the ten-year resident card describes the ordinary card, the three-year absence rule and renewal conditions. The statutory absence provision is CESEDA Article L411-5 on Légifrance; it also distinguishes the wording for long-term resident–EU cards.
