Georgia (the Country): the 183-Day Tax Residency Rule

Georgia’s 183-day test uses rolling 12-calendar-month periods. Its legal count includes special rules for time abroad, excluded visits and days used for a previous tax period. Check your own dates in the calculator.

Last verified: 12 November 2026

In short: Georgia’s ordinary day test requires 183 or more qualifying days in any continuous 12-calendar-month period ending in the tax year. It can make you resident for that whole tax year. The count is not simply a border-crossing total: Article 34 adds specified absences abroad, excludes some time in Georgia, and restricts reuse of days from a previous residency determination.

Threshold
183 qualifying days or more
Counting window
Any continuous 12 calendar months ending in the tax year
Part days
A day in Georgia counts regardless of its length, unless excluded
Adjustments
Specified absences abroad, excluded visits and previously used days
Tax year
Calendar year
Legal basis
Tax Code of Georgia, Article 34

The rule

Under Article 34(2), a person who meets the 183-day test in a continuous 12-calendar-month period ending in the current tax year is resident for the entire current year. The period can cross 1 January. It is not limited to the days physically spent in Georgia during that calendar year.

  • Time that can be added. Article 34(3) includes time spent outside Georgia specifically for treatment, leisure, a business trip or education. Check whether an absence comes within that provision rather than assuming every day abroad breaks the legal count.
  • Time that is excluded. Article 34(4) excludes time in Georgia for treatment or leisure, transit between two foreign countries, and specified diplomatic, consular, international-organisation or foreign-public-service situations. Some exclusions also cover family members; the international-organisation and foreign-public-service provision has an exception for Georgian citizens.

Days are not freely reusable between tax periods. Article 34(8) says that days used to establish residence in the previous tax period are not taken into account when establishing residence for a following period. That condition needs attention when reviewing consecutive years. The statute does not provide a simple border-log formula for assigning those days in every case.

How to count it

  1. List your Georgia stays and any absences abroad that may qualify under Article 34(3), with their dates and reasons.
  2. Count eligible days of presence, including partial days, and apply the statutory additions and exclusions. Do not automatically count a holiday in Georgia as qualifying presence.
  3. Check whether any days were used for the previous tax period’s residency determination before applying the rolling window to another year.
  4. Test continuous 12-calendar-month periods ending in the tax year. Reaching 183 qualifying days or more meets the ordinary day test for that year.

Example. You spend 100 qualifying days in Georgia between September and December 2025, then 90 between February and May 2026. Assume no counted day is excluded or already used for a previous tax period’s residence determination.

The days total 190 inside the period from 1 September 2025 to 31 August 2026. Neither calendar year alone contains 183 of those days, but the continuous 12-month period ends in 2026, so the day test is met for the 2026 tax year.

Use the 183-day calculator to organise recorded days in Georgia. A day calendar alone does not decide the statutory additions, exclusions or reuse of days.

Beyond the day count

Article 34 also covers Georgian public service abroad and provides separate routes outside the ordinary day test, including a high-net-worth procedure. Do not treat a physical-day total below 183 as a universal guarantee of non-residence. Dual residence may also require checking an applicable tax treaty.

FAQ

How many days can you stay in Georgia (the country) without becoming a tax resident?

There is no universal safe number of physical days. The ordinary test uses 183 qualifying days in a continuous 12-calendar-month period ending in the tax year, with statutory additions, exclusions and a restriction on reusing days. Other residence routes also exist.

Is the 183-day rule based on the calendar year?

The ordinary tax year is the calendar year, but the day-count window is any continuous 12 calendar months ending in it. Review the rule on previously used days when testing a later tax period.

Do all days in Georgia count?

No. Article 34(4) excludes specified visits and statuses. Eligible partial days count under Article 34(5), and Article 34(3) can also include specified absences abroad. The legal count can therefore differ from physical days shown in a travel log.

Official sources

Georgia’s Tax Code, Article 34, sets the 183-day test, specified absences abroad, exclusions, part-day counting and the restriction on reusing days from a previous tax period. Article 135(1) sets the ordinary calendar-year accounting period. The Georgian consolidated text contains the corresponding provisions.
