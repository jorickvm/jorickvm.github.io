Netherlands Residence Permit Absence Rules

Two clocks run while you are out of the country: six continuous months can cost a Dutch residence permit, and twelve continuous months outside the European Union ends EU long-term resident status. They are counted differently.

Last verified: September 2026

In short: the IND treats your main residence as moved abroad if you have been outside the Netherlands for more than 6 continuous months, counted within one calendar year, or for more than 4 continuous months in each of 3 consecutive years. Your permit can then be withdrawn or not extended. EU long-term resident status is a separate, more generous rule: it ends after 12 consecutive months outside the European Union, or after 6 years outside the Netherlands.

Rule 1: your Dutch residence permit

Threshold
More than 6 continuous months outside the Netherlands
Also triggers it
More than 4 continuous months in each of 3 consecutive years
Counting window
Within one calendar year (1 January – 31 December)
A day counts as away if
No stay in the Netherlands touches it
Applies to
Temporary and permanent Dutch residence permits, including highly skilled migrants (kennismigranten)
If the rule is met
The IND can withdraw the permit, or decide not to extend it
Legal basis
Vreemdelingenwet 2000, Article 18(1)(a)

A Dutch residence permit assumes the Netherlands is your main residence. The IND does not weigh how attached you feel to the country; it looks at how long you were gone in one run. Two absence patterns move your main residence abroad on their own:

  • More than 6 continuous months outside the Netherlands. The IND counts this per calendar year, and does not add separate absences together.
  • More than 4 continuous months, in each of 3 consecutive years. A pattern of shorter absences reaches the same conclusion over time.

The calendar-year point is the one that catches people out. In the IND's own example, an absence running from November of one year into June of the next does not meet the six-month test, because the months on either side of 1 January are not in the same calendar year.

Rule 2: EU long-term resident status

Status ends after
12 consecutive months outside the European Union
Also ends it
6 years outside the Netherlands
Counting window
The current absence, from the day your last stay ended
Territory counted
The 27 EU member states, the EEA (Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein) and Switzerland
Applies to
Holders of EU long-term resident status granted by the Netherlands
Legal basis
Directive 2003/109/EC, Article 9

EU long-term resident status is European rather than Dutch. It is granted by the Netherlands under Directive 2003/109/EC after five years of legal residence, and it is lost on European terms:

  • 12 consecutive months outside the European Union (Article 9(1)(c)). Days in another member state are days inside, so a year split between Lisbon and Warsaw never starts this clock. A year in Bangkok does.
  • 6 years outside the Netherlands (Article 9(4)), even if all of it was spent inside the EU. The IND's guidance excludes studying in another member state from this six-year count.

Obtaining the status has its own absence limits: across the five qualifying years you may not be outside the Netherlands for more than 6 consecutive months, or for more than 10 months in total (Article 4(3)). AtlasDays tracks the two absences that end the status; the five-year qualifying count you check yourself for now.

Note that the EU is not the Schengen area. Ireland and Cyprus are EU member states and count as inside; Norway, Iceland, Switzerland and Liechtenstein are in Schengen but not the EU. A short-stay allowance runs on the Schengen list instead: see the Schengen 90/180 rule.

How to count it

  1. Log every stay, including the ones at home. A day away is a day that no stay in the Netherlands touches.
  2. Departure and arrival days are days in the country, not days away, because a stay still touches them.
  3. Count the current absence: the days since your last stay in the Netherlands ended, up to today.
  4. Measure that run against 180 days for the permit rule, and against 365 days for long-term resident status, where the run only counts days spent outside all 27 member states.

Example. You leave the Netherlands on 12 March 2025 and return on 5 November 2025. The absence runs from 13 March to 4 November: 237 days. It passes 180 days on 9 September 2025, and the whole run sits inside one calendar year, so the six-month test is met.

Now shift the same trip. You leave on 12 November 2025 and return on 20 June 2026. That is 219 continuous days away, but the IND splits it at 1 January: 49 days fall in 2025 and 170 in 2026, and neither year exceeds six continuous months.

AtlasDays counts an absence end to end, so that second trip shows as one 219-day run and crosses the 180-day limit. Read it as the point to check your dates against the calendar year, not as proof the permit is at risk.

Beyond the day count

The day count is not the only route to losing main residency. The IND also treats moving the centre of your activities abroad as a change of main residence, whatever any single absence added up to.

The two territories are also not described identically. The Directive speaks of absence from the territory of the Community, but the IND, which grants and withdraws the Dutch status, writes the test as absence from the EU, the EEA or Switzerland. AtlasDays follows the IND: its tracker counts days away from the 27 member states plus Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Switzerland, so a month in Oslo or Zurich is not time away. If your status was granted by another member state, check whether that state adds the EEA and Switzerland as the Netherlands does. And if you reached the status through an EU Blue Card, the IND's five-year absence limits are measured from the European Union rather than from the Netherlands: no more than 12 consecutive months, and no more than 18 months in total.

None of this is automatic. Withdrawal is a decision on your file, so if an absence is running close to a limit, or has already passed one, put the question to the IND or an immigration adviser with your actual dates in hand.

FAQ

How long can you leave the Netherlands without losing your residence permit?

Up to 6 continuous months, counted within a single calendar year. Beyond that the IND can treat your main residence as moved abroad and withdraw the permit or refuse to extend it. More than 4 continuous months in each of 3 consecutive years has the same effect.

Does time in another EU country count as an absence?

For the Dutch permit, yes: any day outside the Netherlands is a day away, wherever you spent it. For EU long-term resident status the 12-month clock runs only while you are outside the European Union, although 6 years outside the Netherlands ends the Dutch status regardless of where those years were spent.

Does the six-month absence reset on 1 January?

For this test, in effect yes. The IND counts the 6 continuous months within a calendar year, so an absence from November to June is split at 1 January and neither part exceeds six months. Repeating that pattern can still meet the 4-month test, which looks at 3 consecutive years.

Official sources

"You have been outside the Netherlands for more than 6 continuous months" and "You have been outside the EU, EEA or Switzerland for more than 12 continuous months", per the IND (Main residency), with the five-year absence limits for obtaining the status on IND (Residence permit for long-term EU residents) and the underlying EU law in Directive 2003/109/EC, Article 9.
