Japan Tax Residency: Domicile, Residence and the 183-Day Myth

Japan’s domestic test uses domicile or residence maintained continuously for at least a year. A yearly count of physical days cannot decide either condition. Check your own dates in the calculator below.

Two routes in the domestic definition

Japan’s National Tax Agency classifies an individual as resident if they have a domicile in Japan or have maintained a residence there continuously for one year or more. An individual outside those categories is a non-resident. Counting to 183 is not the general domestic test.

Residence is not just a sum of visits

A residence maintained in Japan can remain relevant during a temporary absence. The NTA’s income-tax guide discusses circumstances such as family remaining in Japan, retaining accommodation for return and keeping personal belongings there. Physical absence does not automatically erase continuous residence.

Keep the visitor and tax questions separate

A temporary visitor’s permitted stay concerns immigration. It does not determine tax residence. Similarly, the separate non-permanent-resident tax category should not be confused with an immigration residence permit or reduced to a one-year travel total.

What your annual record can show

Record actual arrival and departure dates and retain information about your home and temporary absences. For example, 150 recorded days do not establish non-residence if your domicile is in Japan. The log supplies dates, while the tax assessment considers the relevant residence facts.

Why a calendar year can hide the relevant period

Illustrative physical record: 1 July to 31 December 2025 spans 184 days, and 1 January to 30 June 2026 spans 181. The visits form a 365-day record split across two calendar years.

Neither the split nor a 183-day line determines domicile or the legal continuity of residence. The example is not an eligibility date: the relevant residence facts, the precise period and any earlier domicile still require assessment.

A temporary absence is a separate fact

If you travel abroad temporarily, record that trip accurately. Also keep the facts about the residence retained for your return, family arrangements and belongings. The NTA’s guidance explains why physical absence and an end to residence are not automatically the same event.

For that reason, reducing your Japanese physical total by a holiday’s length does not itself demonstrate that a continuous residence period restarted. A counter should preserve the actual travel record without claiming to make that legal decision.

Do not mix up similarly named statuses

Temporary Visitor is an immigration permission. Resident and non-permanent resident in the income-tax guidance are tax classifications. A label on one document should not be substituted for the other test. In particular, the tax category’s multi-year residence history cannot be inferred from a single annual travel total.

Keep your immigration permission, physical travel dates and residence-history evidence as separate but connected records. The tool below helps with the physical dates only, and does not choose a Japanese tax status for you.

FAQ

Does staying below 183 days guarantee non-residence?

No. Japan’s domestic definition is based on domicile or continuous residence, not a universal 183-day threshold.

Does a temporary absence end residence?

Not automatically. The NTA describes circumstances in which residence continues during a temporary absence.

What should a personal day counter assess?

Physical presence only. It should not claim to determine domicile, continuous residence or tax status.
