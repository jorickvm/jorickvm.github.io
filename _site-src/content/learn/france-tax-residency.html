France's Tax Residency Rules

France has no day threshold in law. Your home, your principal stay, your main work or your main economic interests decide. Here is how the principal stay is measured.

Last verified: November 2026

In short: France has no 183-day rule. You are a French tax resident if any one of four things is in France: your home, your principal stay, your main professional activity or the centre of your economic interests. If you have no home anywhere, your principal stay is usually where you spend more than six months of the year. If you spread the year over several countries, France can be your principal stay with far fewer days: it is enough to spend more days in France than in any other single country.

The rule

Article 4 B of the French tax code makes you resident if you have in France your home or your principal stay, your main professional activity, or the centre of your economic interests. The tests are alternatives: meeting one is enough, whatever the others say.

  • Your home comes first. Your home is where you normally live with your family. The principal stay only decides when you have no home anywhere. In a 1995 case, a couple spent about 275 days in mainland France in one year and were still not resident: their home, and their work, stayed in Nouméa.
  • Six months is a rule of thumb. The tax administration treats more than six months in France in a year as a principal stay, but says this is not absolute.
  • Several countries change the count. When you stay in more than two countries in a year, France is your principal stay if your time there is longer than your time in each of the other countries. 120 days can be enough.
  • France means mainland France, Corsica and the overseas departments (Guadeloupe, French Guiana, Martinique, Réunion and Mayotte). French Polynesia, New Caledonia and the other overseas collectivities have their own tax systems and are outside it.

How to count it

  1. First check whether you have a home anywhere. The principal-stay test determines residence only if you do not. Then list every trip in the calendar year, in every country, with arrival and departure dates.
  2. Count your days in France, including the overseas departments.
  3. Count your days in each other country separately.
  4. If France has more than six months, it is your principal stay. If it has fewer, compare it with the single country where you spent the most other days: if France has more, France is your principal stay.

Example. In one year you spend 120 days in France, 100 in Spain, 80 in Portugal and 65 in Italy, and you have no home anywhere.

No country reaches six months, but France has more days than any other single country, so France is your principal stay and you are a French tax resident for that year.

Days you cannot account for are hard to argue about. If you may need to show that France was not your principal stay, keep a record of every day of the year, including the days you spend in your own country.

Beyond the day count

The other three tests do not count days. A home where your family lives in France makes you resident however long you are away. So does professional activity in France, unless you show it is only ancillary, or having your main investments, business or income source in France. In the year you move to France or leave it, the six-month rule does not apply, and special rules for that year (CGI Articles 166 and 167) decide how you are taxed. If another country also treats you as resident, the tax treaty between the two usually decides, looking at your permanent home first, then your personal and economic ties, then where you habitually stay, then your nationality.

FAQ

Is there a 183-day rule in France?

Not in law. If you have no home anywhere, more than six months in France usually makes it your principal stay, but you can become resident with fewer days, and a home, main work or economic interests in France make you resident whatever your days.

Can you be a French tax resident with fewer than 183 days?

Yes. If you spread the year over several countries and have no home anywhere, France is your principal stay as soon as you spend more days there than in any other single country.

Does French tax residence depend on nationality?

No. It depends on where you live, stay, work and hold your interests, not on your passport.
