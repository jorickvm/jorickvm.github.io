US B1/B2 Visa: Understanding the 180-Day Stay Limit

A practical explainer of what the U.S. visitor-visa "180-day limit" shorthand usually means, where it misleads, and how visa validity, admission, and authorized stay differ.

Last verified: June 2026

In short: the "180-day limit" is shorthand for the idea that a B1/B2 visitor may be admitted for up to about six months on a given entry. It is not a guarantee. The visa lets you travel to a port of entry and request admission; CBP decides each arrival, and the admitted-until date on your I-94 is what actually controls how long you may remain.

Three steps people collapse into one

The U.S. system separates things that travelers routinely treat as a single fact:

  • The visa lets you travel to a port of entry and request admission.
  • The admission decision is made by CBP each time you arrive.
  • The authorized stay is the period you may actually remain after that admission.

So "I have a B1/B2 visa" is not the same statement as "I can stay for 180 days." The shorthand points in roughly the right direction, since a visitor is thinking in months rather than in a Schengen-style rolling 90/180 framework. But it does not mean every holder automatically receives 180 days, that six months is guaranteed on every arrival, or that some annual counter resets itself after departure.

Visa validity is not authorized stay

State Department guidance is explicit that the visa expiration date is not the same thing as how long you are authorized to remain in the United States.

  • Visa validity is the period during which the visa can be used to travel to a port of entry and request admission.
  • Authorized stay is the period CBP grants you for that specific visit when you are admitted.
  • The I-94 record is the official reference. The admitted-until date on your admission record is what governs.

A visa valid for several years does not let you remain in the United States for several years at a time. It means you may make repeated trips for the same temporary purpose while the visa remains valid, subject to inspection and admission each time.

Repeated visits and long-stay patterns

The easiest mistake after learning the six-month shorthand is assuming that leaving the United States automatically resets another full stay on demand. There is no general published rule that a B1/B2 visitor gets a guaranteed fresh six months after every departure. Each entry is a new admission decision, and the visitor classification is still built around a temporary visit with an intent to depart afterwards.

Repeated long stays, back-to-back trips, or a pattern that starts to look more like residence than visiting can raise questions at the border or in later visa processing. That is not a numeric cut-off for "too many visits." It means the pattern matters, and the shorthand stops being enough.

The safe approach: do not plan around the assumption that a B1/B2 visa guarantees six months on every trip, and do not treat the visa expiration date as the date that controls how long you may remain.

Where to check, and where to stop

  • For what a visa does and does not do, start with the State Department's What the Visa Expiration Date Means page.
  • For visitor-visa purpose and temporary-visit framing, use the State Department's Visitor Visa guidance.
  • For your admitted-until date, check the official I-94 record.
  • If you need to stay longer than your current admission allows, review USCIS Extend Your Stay guidance before that period expires.

This page explains concepts and is not case-specific immigration advice. The official record for your stay is not your memory of what usually happens; it is the admission decision and the record attached to that entry.
