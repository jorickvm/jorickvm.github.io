Connecticut's 183-Day Statutory Residency Rule

Connecticut’s statutory test requires more than 183 counted days and a permanent place of abode kept for the entire taxable year. Domicile and specific nonresident exceptions are separate.

Last verified: 12 November 2026

In short: Connecticut treats you as a statutory resident if you both maintain a permanent place of abode in the state for the entire taxable year and spend more than 183 days (184 or more) in Connecticut during that year. This applies even if your permanent home is in another state or country. Any part of a day in Connecticut counts as a Connecticut day, except time spent solely in transit to a destination outside Connecticut.

The rule

Connecticut has two separate ways to be a resident: by domicile (Connecticut is your permanent legal home) or by statutory residence. The 183-day rule is the statutory-residence test, and it has two conditions that must both be met:

  • A permanent place of abode. You permanently maintain a residence in Connecticut for the entire taxable year. It can be owned or leased by you or your spouse. A place kept only for a temporary stay to accomplish a particular purpose is not a permanent abode.
  • More than 183 days. You spend more than 183 Connecticut days during the taxable year, that is 184 or more. Exactly 183 days keeps you under the day test.

Meet both and you are a statutory resident, taxed by Connecticut on your income as a resident, even if you are domiciled in another state or country.

How to count it

  1. Confirm you maintained a permanent place of abode in Connecticut for the entire taxable year.
  2. Count every day on which you were present in Connecticut for any part of the day.
  3. Exclude parts of days spent in the state solely in transit to a destination outside Connecticut.
  4. Add the Connecticut days across the whole taxable year. Days do not need to be consecutive.
  5. If the total is more than 183 and the place-of-abode condition is met, you are a statutory resident for that year.

Example. You are domiciled in Florida but keep an apartment in Stamford all year. Across the year you are physically in Connecticut on 190 days, many of them just for part of the day.

Assuming none qualifies for the transit exception and no special status exception applies, all 190 days count. With a permanent place of abode plus more than 183 days, you are a Connecticut statutory resident, taxed as a resident, despite being a Florida domiciliary.

Beyond the day count

The 183-day test is only the statutory route. Domicile can establish residence independently, while certain Connecticut domiciliaries qualify for nonresident treatment under the separate Group A or foreign-presence Group B exceptions. Those exceptions have their own abode, presence and family conditions. The statutory-residence definition also excludes a nondomiciliary in active service in the US armed forces. Keep records supporting the Connecticut days you claim; the regulation requires them when a nonresident with a Connecticut abode is examined.

FAQ

How many days can you spend in Connecticut without becoming a statutory resident?

At most 183 counted days stays below this statutory day threshold. More than 183, together with a permanent place of abode kept for the entire taxable year and no applicable exception, meets the test. Domicile remains separate.

What counts as a day in Connecticut?

Any part of a day counts, except a part-day spent solely in transit to a destination outside Connecticut. Simply describing a journey as an airport trip does not by itself establish that exception.

Do you need a home in Connecticut to be caught by the rule?

For the statutory test, yes. Without a permanent place of abode there is no statutory residence on days alone, though separate domicile rules can still make you a resident.
