Maryland's 183-Day Statutory Residency Rule

Maryland’s statutory test combines a place of abode kept for more than six months with at least 183 counted days in the taxable year. A short stay across midnight is not automatically two days.

Last verified: 12 November 2026

In short: Maryland treats you as a statutory resident if you both maintain a place of abode in the state for more than six months of the taxable year and are physically present in Maryland for 183 days or more during the year. This applies even if your permanent home is elsewhere. Any part of a day can count, but a continuous period of 24 hours or less cannot count as more than one day.

The rule

Maryland has two separate ways to be a resident: by domicile (Maryland is your true, permanent home, with separate rules for moves during the year) or by statutory residence. The 183-day rule is the statutory-residence test, and it has two conditions that must both be met:

  • A place of abode for more than six months. You maintain a place to live in Maryland for more than six months of the taxable year. Owning property alone does not settle this condition or the separate presence test.
  • 183 days or more. You are physically present in Maryland for 183 days or more during the taxable year. Unlike some states, Maryland's threshold is met at exactly 183 days, not one day above it.

Meeting both brings you within the statutory-resident test in Maryland’s regulation and published guidance, even if you are domiciled elsewhere. Resident status, whether a return is required and any reciprocity relief are separate questions.

How to count it

  1. Confirm you maintained a place of abode in Maryland for more than six months of the year.
  2. Count presence using the partial-day rule, but do not count a continuous period of 24 hours or less as more than one day.
  3. Add the counted days across the taxable year. They do not need to be consecutive. Keep arrival and departure times for short overnight visits.
  4. If the total reaches 183 or more and the place-of-abode condition is met, you are a statutory resident for that year.

Example. You arrive in Maryland at 10 p.m. on Monday and leave at 8 a.m. on Tuesday. That is a continuous ten-hour visit. It touches two calendar dates but cannot count as more than one Maryland day.

Separately, keeping an abode in Maryland for more than six months and accumulating 183 counted days meets the two-part test. Apply the 24-hour cap before comparing your total with 183.

Beyond the day count

The 183-day test is only one route. Domicile can establish residence independently, and moving into or out of Maryland has part-year rules. Military members and their spouses have special protections. Reciprocity can also change how wages are taxed; Maryland’s agreement with West Virginia can apply regardless of time spent in the other state. Keep residence status separate from the return and tax treatment of a particular income source.

FAQ

How many days can you spend in Maryland without becoming a statutory resident?

Fewer than 183 counted days stays below the presence part of this test. At least 183 counted days plus a place of abode kept for more than six months meets it. Domicile can establish residence separately.

What counts as a day in Maryland?

Any part of a day can count, but the regulation limits a continuous period of 24 hours or less to no more than one day. A short cross-midnight stay is therefore not automatically two counted days.

Do you need a home in Maryland to be caught by the rule?

For the statutory test, yes. Without a place of abode kept more than six months there is no statutory residence on days alone, though separate domicile rules can still make you a resident.
