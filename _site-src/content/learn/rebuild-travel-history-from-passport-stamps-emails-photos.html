How to Rebuild Your Travel History From Passport Stamps, Emails, and Photos

A method for reconstructing past trips, and proving how long you spent in a country, when your own log is incomplete or missing: which evidence to start from, how to handle gaps and conflicts, and how to end up with one record you can defend.

Last verified: September 2026

In short: start from the strongest dated record of each border crossing, ideally an official entry and exit record, then corroborate with bookings, card statements and photos. Mark anything you cannot pin to a day as approximate, write down why each gap exists, and keep one chronology. For the Schengen area, stamps stop helping after 10 April 2026: non-EU travelers are recorded in the EU Entry/Exit System instead of being stamped, and you can ask for that record.

The method: anchor, corroborate, mark, explain, consolidate

  1. Write down what you remember first. List trips by year before you open your inbox. Doing it in that order stops the first email you find from quietly reshaping your memory, and it gives you a checklist of trips that still need evidence.
  2. Anchor each border crossing. For every trip, find the strongest record of the day you entered and the day you left: an official movement record, a stamp, or a boarding pass. Where you can, request official records early, because they can take weeks (see how to get your official entry and exit records).
  3. Corroborate around the anchors. Hotel nights should sit inside the travel window, card charges should cluster in the right country, and photo dates should match the place. Each consistent record makes the anchor harder to doubt.
  4. Mark approximations honestly. If you know the year but not the days, record the year and say so. One invented exact date inside an otherwise careful record does more damage than an honest "dates not established".
  5. Explain every gap. A missing leg is rarely fatal if you can say why it is missing and bound it between two firm dates. An unexplained hole invites the question you least want asked.
  6. Keep one chronology. Every form, adviser and audit should get the same version. Two lists that describe the same trip differently are harder to repair than one list with a disclosed gap.

For each trip, keep five things together: the country, the dates, the evidence each date rests on, its precision (exact or approximate), and any unresolved gap. That structure is what turns a folder of documents into a record someone else can check.

Which evidence is strongest

No single source settles everything. What matters is whether a record fixes the day you crossed a border, or only shows you were somewhere at some point.

1. Official movement records

Examples
EU Entry/Exit System record, US I-94 travel history, Canada's Travel History Report, Australian movement records
What it establishes
Entry and exit dates recorded by the border authority itself
Where it stops
Covers only that country's border; land crossings and older years can be patchy; can take weeks to arrive

2. Stamps and proof of travel

Examples
Passport stamps, boarding passes, airline receipts for flown segments, carrier records
What it establishes
The day you crossed or flew
Where it stops
Stamps can be faint, missing or no longer issued; a boarding pass proves boarding, not where you went next

3. Bookings and invoices

Examples
E-tickets, hotel folios, rental car agreements, rail and ferry tickets
What it establishes
Planned travel, and nights spent at a place
Where it stops
A booking shows intent, not travel; changed or flexible tickets may not reflect the day you actually moved

4. Everyday traces

Examples
Card statements, geotagged photos, phone location history, roaming texts, messages
What it establishes
Presence somewhere on a given day
Where it stops
One moment, not a period; card posting dates lag; device clocks and time zones can shift a date by one day

Photos deserve one warning. A photo's timestamp is the device clock, and a picture taken in Lisbon at 00:30 local time can show the previous day if it is read in another time zone. Photos you received from someone else, saved from a chat or a shared album, show their trip, not yours.

Passport stamps no longer anchor Schengen travel

For years, passport stamps were the usual way to rebuild Schengen trips. That has changed. The EU Entry/Exit System (EES) registers non-EU travelers electronically at the external border, recording the date and place of each entry and exit together with biometric data, and replaces manual stamping. It started on 12 October 2025 with a phased rollout and has been fully operational at all external border crossings since 10 April 2026.

Two consequences for a reconstruction:

  • Before 12 October 2025: stamps are still your main official Schengen evidence. Photograph every page of your current and old passports before you renew or lose them.
  • From 12 October 2025, and for every crossing from 10 April 2026: expect an electronic record instead of a stamp. You can request the data EES holds about you. The EU's online stay checker reads your EES record and shows how many days you have left, but it does not list past crossings or count stays that began before 10 April 2026.

Stamps were never complete evidence anyway: travel inside the Schengen area has no border checks, so a trip from France to Italy leaves no official trace. Internal movements always rest on bookings, invoices and everyday records.

A worked reconstruction

An exact trip. You need your 2024 days in Portugal. Your inbox has an e-ticket from London to Lisbon on 3 June and a return booked for 20 June. A later airline email moved the return to 21 June, and your card statement shows a Lisbon café charge on the morning of 21 June. Hotel invoices cover the nights of 3–20 June, and your photos from Porto are dated 10–12 June. Record Portugal, 3–21 June 2024, and note that the return date rests on the change email plus the card charge, not the original booking. Counting both the arrival and departure day, as the Schengen rule does, that is 19 days.

An approximate trip. You remember a week in Morocco in 2019, but the only evidence is a handful of photos with no location data and a card charge in dirhams posted in April. Record Morocco, 2019, dates not established, with a note on what the evidence shows. Do not turn "probably mid-April" into 12–19 April: that trip should support "I visited Morocco in 2019", not a day count.

When records disagree

Work down the ranking above: an official record beats a stamp, a stamp or boarding pass beats a booking, and a booking beats a card charge. A change email beats the original booking it changed. Where two records of similar strength conflict and you cannot resolve it, record the date you think is right, note the other one, and say why. A disclosed inconsistency is easy to explain later. One discovered by someone else looks like concealment.

The same test catches the most common weakness in a rebuilt record: overclaiming. A hotel invoice supports nights in a hotel, not the whole trip. A card charge in Paris supports one day in France, not a week. Ask of every document what it actually proves, and stop there.

FAQ

How can I prove how long I spent in a country without stamps?

Anchor each entry and exit with the strongest dated record you have, such as an official movement record, a boarding pass or an airline receipt, then corroborate the days in between with hotel invoices, card statements and photos. Mark anything you cannot date exactly as approximate.

Do Schengen countries still stamp passports?

Not for most non-EU travelers. The EU Entry/Exit System replaced manual stamping at the external border, starting on 12 October 2025 and fully operational since 10 April 2026. Your entries and exits are recorded electronically, and you can request that data.

What if I only remember the year of a trip?

Record the year and say the dates are not established. Do not invent days. An approximate trip can support that you visited a country, but it should not be used in a day count until a document confirms the dates.
