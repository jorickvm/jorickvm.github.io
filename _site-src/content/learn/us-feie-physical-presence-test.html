FEIE Physical Presence Test: 330 Full Days Abroad

US citizens and residents living abroad can exclude foreign earnings from US tax by spending 330 full days in foreign countries in any 12 months. Here is what counts as a full day.

Last verified: November 2026

In short: you meet the physical presence test if you are in a foreign country or countries for at least 330 full days during any 12 consecutive months. A full day runs from midnight to midnight, so the days you spend travelling to or from the US do not count, and neither do days in US territories or at sea. That leaves at most 35 other days in the period, or 36 when it includes 29 February. You also need your tax home in a foreign country.

The rule

The foreign earned income exclusion lets a US citizen or resident leave foreign earned income, up to an annual maximum that is adjusted for inflation, out of their US taxable income. It is claimed on Form 2555. To qualify you need a tax home in a foreign country and you must pass one of two tests. The physical presence test is the one that counts days: at least 330 full days in a foreign country or countries during any period of 12 consecutive months. The days do not have to be consecutive, and they can be spent abroad for any reason, including holidays.

  • A full day is midnight to midnight. A day you spend partly in the US, in a US territory or on international waters is not a full day abroad.
  • The 12 months can start on any day. A period begins on any day of the month and ends the day before the same date 12 months later. It does not have to match the tax year, and it does not have to start on your first full day abroad.
  • A foreign country is another government's territory. That includes its territorial waters, up to 12 nautical miles out, and its airspace. Puerto Rico, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, the US Virgin Islands and American Samoa are not foreign countries for the exclusion. Neither are Antarctica or international waters.

How travel days count

Moving between foreign countries costs nothing, as long as any part of the journey outside a foreign country takes less than 24 hours. Travel to or from the US is different: time on or over international waters is not time in a foreign country, so the travel days drop out.

No full days lost

A connection through a US airport between two foreign countries, if you are in the US for less than 24 hours, does not make you present in the US. The connection counts as travel outside a foreign country, so the 24-hour limit above applies to it.

How to count it

  1. Mark every day you spent at least partly in the US, in a US territory or at sea.
  2. Mark the travel days around those stays: the day you left a foreign country and the day you arrived in one, when part of the day was spent outside any foreign country.
  3. Pick a 12-month period. With a complete record of excluded time, every unmarked day in it is a full day abroad. Missing records are not evidence of foreign presence.
  4. If any 12-month period holds 330 or more of them, the day count is met for that period.

Example. You live in Lisbon and fly to New York on 1 April, landing the same day. You fly back overnight on 10 April and land in Lisbon on 11 April.

1 to 10 April are US days and 11 April is a travel day: 11 days that are not full days abroad. In a 12-month period without 29 February, that leaves 24 more such days before the period falls below 330.

Beyond the day count

The days alone do not qualify you. Your tax home must be in a foreign country for the days to count. It usually means the area where you mainly work, which is separate from tax residence or your family home. The exclusion covers earned income only, not investment income. Pay for work done in international waters or airspace is not foreign earned income either. The alternative bona fide residence test requires genuine residence in a foreign country for an uninterrupted period that includes an entire tax year. It is available to US citizens and to US resident aliens who are citizens or nationals of a country with a US income tax treaty. Both tests, and how to claim the exclusion, are in IRS Publication 54.

FAQ

How many days can I spend in the US and still pass?

At most 35 days in the 12-month period that are not full days abroad, or 36 when the period includes 29 February. Travel days to and from the US use up that allowance too, and so do days in US territories.

Does the 12-month period have to be the calendar year?

No. It can begin on any day of the month and runs for 12 consecutive months, so it rarely matches the tax year. You choose the period.

Do days in Puerto Rico or Guam count as days abroad?

No. Puerto Rico, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, the US Virgin Islands and American Samoa are US territories, not foreign countries, for the exclusion.
