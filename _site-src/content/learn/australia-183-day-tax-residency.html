Australia's 183-Day Tax Residency Rule

Australia’s “183-day test” means more than half the income year: 183 days in an ordinary year, but 184 in a leap income year. Its visitor exception and the other residence tests still matter.

Last verified: 12 November 2026

In short: the day test applies when you are in Australia for more than half of its 1 July–30 June income year. That is 183 days in a 365-day year or 184 in a 366-day year. The test does not apply if the Commissioner is satisfied both that your usual place of abode is outside Australia and that you do not intend to take up residence in Australia. Another residence test can still apply.

More than half the income year: 183 days, or 184 in a leap income year

The rule

The 183-day test is one of four alternative individual residence tests. It expands the ordinary meaning of residence but does not replace the ordinary concepts, domicile or Commonwealth superannuation tests.

  • Use the income year. Add presence from 1 July to 30 June, not January to December. There is no rolling 12-month window for this test.
  • Use the correct majority. Separate stays combine. ATO guidance counts part of a day as a whole day for administrative ease. The threshold is more than half of the particular income year, so a year containing 29 February needs 184 days rather than 183.
  • Check both exception conditions. The Commissioner must be satisfied that your usual place of abode is overseas and that you do not intend to take up residence here. A genuine visitor can remain outside this test despite an extended stay.

How to count it

  1. Choose the income year from 1 July to 30 June.
  2. List every Australian stay in that period and count partial days, including arrival and departure days.
  3. Add separate visits, counting an overlapping day only once, and compare the total with 183 days in an ordinary income year or 184 in a leap income year.
  4. Review the usual-abode and intention conditions and the other residence tests before deciding your status.

Example. The income year from 1 July 2023 to 30 June 2024 has 366 days. A presence total of 183 days is exactly half, not more than half; 184 meets the numerical threshold.

Meeting that number still leaves the visitor exception to consider. Nor does a lower number establish non-residence under the other tests.

Beyond the day count

The resides and domicile tests can establish residence with fewer days. The Commonwealth superannuation test applies to specified schemes and related people, not every Australian superannuation account.

Residence need not cover the whole income year merely because the day test is met. ATO Ruling TR 2023/1, paragraph 105, says the start and end depend on the facts and circumstances. An applicable tax treaty can also affect how two countries allocate taxing rights.

FAQ

Does 183 days always meet Australia’s day test?

No. The legal threshold is more than half the income year: 183 days in an ordinary 365-day year, but 184 in a 366-day income year. The visitor exception can still apply after the numerical threshold is met.

Is the 183-day test based on the calendar year?

No. It uses the income year from 1 July to 30 June. Separate stays are added within that period.

Does staying below the day threshold guarantee non-residence?

No. Another residence test can apply with fewer days. The ordinary concepts, domicile and specified Commonwealth superannuation tests must also be considered.
