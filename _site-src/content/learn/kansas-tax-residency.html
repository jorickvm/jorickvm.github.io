Kansas's Six-Month Residency Presumption

Spending more than six months of a tax year in Kansas, in total, presumes you are a Kansas resident. The presumption can be rebutted. Here is how the count works.

Last verified: November 2026

In short: Kansas residents are people domiciled in Kansas. On top of that, anyone who spends, in the aggregate, more than six months of the taxable year in the state is presumed to be a resident, in the absence of proof to the contrary. Kansas does not say how many days make six months, so the cautious reading treats anything past 180 days as more than six months.

The rule

Kansas defines a resident as a person domiciled in the state. The six-month rule is a presumption laid over that test:

  • More than six months, in the aggregate. The time does not need to be continuous. No home in Kansas is needed for the presumption.
  • Rebuttable. Proof that your domicile is elsewhere can overcome it.

Below six months the presumption does not arise, but a Kansas domicile still makes you a resident.

How to count it

  1. Count every day you spent in Kansas, including days you were there for only part of the day.
  2. Add the days across the taxable year, 1 January to 31 December for most people. They do not need to be consecutive.
  3. Kansas states the rule in months, not days. If your total is past 180 days, treat it as more than six months.

Example. You are domiciled in Colorado and work on a project in Wichita from 1 February to 31 August, flying home some weekends. Your days in Kansas add up to 190.

That is more than six months, so Kansas presumes you are a resident. Your Colorado domicile and the temporary assignment are the proof you would offer against it.

Beyond the day count

The presumption only decides who carries the burden. A person domiciled in Kansas is a resident at any day count, and a person who spends more than six months in Kansas while clearly domiciled elsewhere can rebut the presumption with that proof.

FAQ

How long can you stay in Kansas without being presumed a resident?

Up to six months of the taxable year, in total. Kansas does not convert the months into days, so staying at 180 days or fewer is the cautious course.

Is Kansas's six-month rule automatic?

No. It is a presumption that applies in the absence of proof to the contrary, such as a domicile clearly elsewhere.

Do you need a home in Kansas for the six-month rule to apply?

No. The presumption rests on time in the state alone.
