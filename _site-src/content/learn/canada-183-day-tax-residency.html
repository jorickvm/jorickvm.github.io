Canada's 183-Day Tax Residency Rule

Canada’s 183-day rule counts temporary stays, not every border crossing. Residential ties and tax-treaty residence can change the answer.

Last verified: 12 November 2026

In short: a person who is not factually resident can be a deemed resident after 183 or more days of sojourning in one calendar year. Any part of a qualifying day counts. Sojourning means a temporary stay; it does not automatically include every day physically in Canada. Significant residential ties can establish residence with fewer days, and a tax treaty can instead make someone a deemed non-resident.

Two main residence questions for travelers

  • Factual residence. Significant residential ties, such as a dwelling place, spouse or common-law partner, or dependants in Canada, can establish residence regardless of the 183-day count. The facts and the period during which you are resident matter.
  • Deemed residence through sojourning. If you are not factually resident but sojourn in Canada for 183 days or more in a calendar year, paragraph 250(1)(a) can treat you as resident for the entire year. This remains subject to the tax-treaty rule in subsection 250(5).

Special deemed-resident rules also cover some government and military service abroad. The 183-day route is not the whole definition of Canadian residence.

The 183-day rule

Sojourning is a temporary stay, which can be very short. Its nature matters: the CRA does not automatically treat every day or part day physically in Canada as a sojourning day.

  • Use the calendar year. Add qualifying days from 1 January to 31 December. This is not a rolling 12-month test.
  • Add separate stays. Sojourning days need not be consecutive. Distinct visits in the same year combine.
  • Count part days correctly. Any part of a sojourning day counts as a full day, including qualifying arrival and departure days.

Commuting is different. Someone who travels into Canada for work and returns each night to their normal home outside Canada is not sojourning on those commuting days. A vacation stay in Canada can count. Do not decide from a border-crossing total alone.

How to count it

  1. List your Canada stays in the calendar year.
  2. Determine which are sojourning days, rather than automatically including every crossing or commuting day.
  3. Count any part of each qualifying day once and add the separate stays.
  4. If the total reaches 183 days or more, review deemed residence alongside factual residence and the applicable treaty before treating the count as the final answer.

Example. You spend 120 days on temporary stays in Canada between January and May, then 70 more between September and November of the same year. Assume these are sojourning days, you are not factually resident, and no treaty deems you non-resident.

The 190 days meet the threshold, so paragraph 250(1)(a) treats you as resident for that whole year.

Why residency matters

A deemed resident under the sojourner rule is liable for Canadian tax on worldwide income for the whole year, subject to the treaty rule. A factual resident can instead be resident for only part of a year, with worldwide-income taxation for that resident period. Non-residents are generally taxed on certain Canadian-source income.

Under subsection 250(5), a person otherwise resident in Canada is deemed non-resident when an applicable treaty’s residence rules assign residence to the other country. This can change the result even after the domestic day test is met.

FAQ

Does staying below 183 days guarantee Canadian non-residence?

No. Fewer than 183 sojourning days stays below this day threshold, but significant residential ties or another statutory rule can establish residence. A physical-presence total also needs to distinguish sojourning from commuting.

What is the difference between a deemed resident and a factual resident?

A factual resident has sufficient residential ties to Canada. The 183-day deemed-resident route applies to a person who is not factually resident but has enough days of temporary stay. The treaty rule can deem either type non-resident in relevant circumstances.

Is the 183-day rule based on the calendar year?

Yes. Qualifying days are added within 1 January to 31 December, not a rolling 12-month period. They need not be consecutive.
