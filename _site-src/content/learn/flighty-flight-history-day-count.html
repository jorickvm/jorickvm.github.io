How to Turn Your Flighty Flight History Into a Visa and Residency Day Count

Flighty is built to track your flights. Visa limits and residency tests are about days in a country. Here is how to bridge the two without losing accuracy.

Last verified: September 2026

In short: Flighty keeps an excellent log of the flights you have taken, but it does not total how many days you have spent in each country or check that against a visa or residency limit. To get a day count, export your flights as a CSV, treat each arrival as the start of a stay and the next departure from that country as its end, and set aside short connections as transit. A day-counting app can do this straight from the export, but only for the flights that are actually in your Flighty history.

Why your flight history is a strong starting point

Flighty logs the flights you take and turns your history into stats like airports visited, distance flown, and time in the air. For remembering exactly where and when you flew, it is hard to beat. What it is not built to do is answer the questions visa and residency rules ask: how many days you have spent in the Schengen area in the last 180 days, whether you have been in a country more than 183 days this year, or whether your next trip crosses a stay limit. Those are about presence measured in days per country, not about flights. Your flight history is the raw material; the day count is a separate calculation built on top of it.

It is good raw material. Each international flight is a dated border crossing: the destination airport tells you which country you entered, and your next departure tells you when you left by air. A reasonably complete flight history is close to a skeleton of your movements, already dated and far more reliable than counting from memory. It is one of the better sources when you are rebuilding a travel history from passport stamps, emails, and photos. The gap is everything between the flights: how long you actually stayed, whether a connection should count, and any travel you did on the ground.

Check how complete your Flighty history is first

A day count built from Flighty can only be as complete as Flighty's log. Flights you added as you traveled are there. Older flights are there only if you imported them, and as of September 2026 Flighty's own help page says flights from the past year are free to import, while older flights require Flighty Pro. If your history before that point never made it into Flighty, the export has a hole, and so will every count built from it. Fill those years from other records before you rely on a multi-year total.

What has to happen to get from flights to days

  1. Airport to country. Each airport code maps to a country, so an arrival into CDG is an arrival in France and an arrival into NRT is an arrival in Japan.
  2. Chaining legs into trips. A journey is usually several flights. An arrival in a country and the next departure from the same country bracket one stay; domestic flights in between belong to that stay rather than becoming separate trips.
  3. Connections versus stays. A short connection through a third country is normally transit and should not add days there. A long layover is a judgement call, and what matters is whether you passed through immigration. See Does a Layover Count as Visiting a Country?
  4. Counting against a window. Once you have dated stays per country, the count is measured against the relevant rule: a rolling 180-day window for Schengen 90/180, which is a lookback across the whole Schengen area rather than per country, or a calendar or fiscal year for many 183-day residency tests, each with their own exceptions.

One trip, three countries. You fly Amsterdam to Istanbul, landing at 14:00 on 3 May, and leave for Bangkok at 21:30 the same day. You land in Bangkok on 4 May and fly out on 20 May, landing in Doha at 21:30 and leaving for Amsterdam at 07:45 on 21 May.

The 7 hour 30 minute stop in Istanbul is a connection: Türkiye gets a transit record and zero days. Thailand is a stay from 4 to 20 May, which is 17 days when both the arrival and departure dates count. The Doha stop lasts 10 hours 15 minutes, which is long enough to be read as a short stay in Qatar on 20–21 May. If you stayed airside, change it to transit before you trust any Qatar count.

Two ways to do it

Manually. Flighty's settings let you export your flights as a CSV. Open it in a spreadsheet, pair each arrival with the next departure from the same country, and tally the days. It is doable, but it has to be redone every time plans change, and one missed row quietly changes every total built on it. How to Track Days in Each Country compares this with the other methods.

With an import. Bring the same export into an app that infers trips for you. AtlasDays reads the Flighty CSV under Settings → Import → Flighty and builds a preview before anything is saved:

  • It maps airports to countries, chains the legs into stays, and skips canceled flights.
  • A stop of under 10 hours becomes a Transit record, which adds no days to any count. A stop of 10 to 24 hours becomes a stay with a long-transit warning, so you decide what it was.
  • Where an arrival or departure flight is missing, for example because you left by train, the trip is listed as incomplete so you can supply the missing date instead of accepting a guessed one.
  • Duplicates of trips already in your history, and overlaps with them, are flagged in the preview.
  • Each trip's notes get the flight number, route, and time of the flights behind it, so the evidence stays attached to the dates.
  • For US airports, the states are detected from the flights. Tracking each state separately needs AtlasDays Pro; the import itself is free.

The step-by-step is in Help Center: Flighty Import. Import the original export file: a CSV that has been opened and resaved in a spreadsheet may no longer match Flighty's format.

Either way, review before you trust it. An automated pass turns hundreds of flights into trips in seconds, but a connection misread as a stay, or a missing leg, changes the count. Treat the first result as a strong draft and check the trips that feed a limit you care about.

Where flight history alone is not enough

Visa and residency questions are high-stakes, so it is worth being honest about what a flight log cannot tell you:

  • Ground crossings are invisible. A train from France to Spain or a drive across a land border never appears in a flight history, so a stay that ended on the ground needs its dates set by hand.
  • Flights show movement, not where you slept. A connection is not a visit, and a long layover is a grey area. What actually counts as a "day" depends on the rule. See What Counts as a 'Day' for Visa Purposes?
  • It is evidence, not proof. A flight history is strong supporting evidence of your movements, but official decisions still rest on stamps, permits, and authority records. If you are assembling a record for an application, see How to Prepare Travel History for a Visa Application.

The point of starting from Flighty is that you build the record once, then check the same clean set of dated trips against whichever limit applies.

FAQ

Does Flighty count your days in a country?

No. Flighty logs the flights you take and builds flight stats, but it does not total how many days you have spent in each country or measure that against a visa or residency limit. Day counting is a separate calculation that uses your flight history as its source.

Can Flighty track the Schengen 90/180 rule?

Not directly. Flighty is a flight tracker, not a visa-limit calculator, so it does not keep a rolling 90/180 Schengen total. You can use your flight history as the source data and count it against the rule in a spreadsheet or a day-tracking app.

How do I export my travel history from Flighty?

Flighty's settings let you export your flights as a CSV file, which you can save to Files or iCloud Drive. Keep the original file unedited, then open a copy in a spreadsheet or import the original into a day-counting app that turns the flights into dated trips.
