UK Citizenship: The 450-Day and 90-Day Absence Limits

British naturalisation checks both your longer travel history and your most recent year. Here is how the two absence limits work, which places count as presence, and why the application date matters.

Last verified: October 2026

In short: the standard five-year route normally permits up to 450 whole days of absence over five years and 90 in the final 12 months. If you are married to, or in a civil partnership with, a British citizen, the limits are 270 days over three years and the same 90 in the final 12 months. On either route both limits apply, and the final year is part of the total, not an additional allowance. Departure and return dates are not absence days.

Start with the right naturalisation route

One fact on the day you apply decides the route: whether you are married to, or in a civil partnership with, a British citizen. If you are, you apply under section 6(2), the three-year route. Everyone else uses the five-year route under section 6(1). Both routes count absences the same way and both have a final-year limit of 90 days. The length of the period, its overall allowance and the status rules differ.

Standard route, section 6(1)

Qualifying period
Five years up to the application date

No more than 450 days in the five years and 90 in the final 12 months
Status
Indefinite leave to remain or settled status, normally held for 12 months before you apply
Future plans
You must intend to make the UK your main home, or to work abroad in Crown or similar service

Spouse or civil-partner route, section 6(2)

Qualifying period
Three years up to the application date
Absences
No more than 270 days in the three years and 90 in the final 12 months
Status
Indefinite leave to remain or settled status on the application date, with no waiting period
Future plans
No requirement to intend to live in the UK

Both routes also require you to have been in the UK on the first day of the qualifying period, to have had no unlawful residence during it, and to meet the language, Life in the UK and good character requirements.

How the spouse or civil-partner route works

The relationship is tested on the application date: you must be married to, or the civil partner of, a British citizen when the Home Office receives your application. There is no minimum length of marriage, and no wait after you receive indefinite leave to remain or settled status. You can apply once both are true and your three-year record fits the limits.

The status must be indefinite on that date. Limited leave, such as a partner visa, does not qualify, and the Home Office has no discretion to waive this. If the marriage or civil partnership has ended before you apply, or your partner has died, this route is closed and the standard route applies instead. Its five-year window can take in older trips that the three-year window never looked at.

A three-year record. An application received on 15 March 2027 looks back to 16 March 2024. Suppose the first two years of that period hold 190 absence days and the final 12 months hold 80. The total is 270, exactly at the limit, and the final year is within 90.

The same trips on the standard route. If the marriage had ended before the application, the five-year window would start on 16 March 2022. With 160 more absence days in those two earlier years, the total becomes 430. That is still within 450, but the result now depends on years the spouse route did not count.

If your British partner works abroad for the UK government or a closely linked organisation, and was recruited for that service in the UK, the Home Office can waive the three-year residence and absence requirements. That needs the employer's support and normally a marriage or civil partnership of at least three years.

Two limits that must be checked together

For the standard route, compare the total absence in five years with 450 days, then compare the final 12 months with 90 days. Returning between trips does not reset either total. Nor can a quiet earlier year compensate for exceeding the final-year limit.

Exactly at both limits. A record with 360 absent days in the earlier four years and 90 in the final year gives 450 overall and 90 in the final year. Both ordinary numerical limits are met.

Only the final year is over. Change the record to 300 earlier days and 91 recent days. The five-year total is 391, but the final-year total exceeds 90.

Only the overall period is over. With 370 earlier days and 85 recent days, the five-year total is 455. The final year is within its allowance, while the longer period is not.

The periods follow the application date. Moving that date can move old absences out of a window and recent absences into it. Use calendar years and months, including leap days, rather than assuming five years always equals 1,825 days.

Count whole days, not the travel dates

Home Office guidance excludes the dates on which you leave and return. Only full days of absence between them contribute to the total. Keep exact departure and return dates, even for short trips: one day can change a result close to a limit.

  1. Identify the relevant qualifying period and its final 12 months.
  2. List each departure and return, including absences that cross either period boundary.
  3. Count only whole absent days inside each period. Exclude the departure and return dates.
  4. Add the absences separately for the longer period and final year, then check each total against its own allowance.

One journey. Leave on 1 June and return on 10 June. The absent dates are 2–9 June, giving eight days. Leaving on 1 June and returning on 2 June gives zero whole absence days.

A journey crossing a window boundary. If the final-year period begins on 6 January, an absence from 2–10 January contributes five dates to that period: 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10 January. The earlier dates may still count in the longer qualifying period. This assumes those dates are whole absence days, with departure before 2 January and return after 10 January.

Why the Channel Islands and Isle of Man count

For nationality law, the relevant UK territory includes the Channel Islands and Isle of Man. These Crown Dependencies are not part of the UK in the usual geographical sense, but days there count as presence for this test. This includes the Bailiwicks of Jersey and Guernsey, as well as the Isle of Man.

The Republic of Ireland and Gibraltar are outside this territory. Do not treat a stay there as UK presence merely because of Common Travel Area arrangements or British nationality connections. For an itinerary involving several places on the same date, the question is whether the entire day was spent outside the included territory.

The first day is a separate requirement

A low absence total does not establish presence at the beginning of the qualifying period. Form AN gives an example: for an application received on 5 January 2022 under the standard route, the applicant should have been present on 6 January 2017. On the spouse route the date lies three years back: in its example, an application received on 5 May 2021 needs presence on 6 May 2018. Check the precise starting date for the intended application, rather than relying on a rough anniversary.

If you were abroad on that day, moving the application date usually solves it. The Home Office can treat the requirement as met only in special circumstances, such as illness or pandemic travel restrictions at the start of the period. Before submitting, review both the totals and that starting-day record. A change to the application date means reviewing the periods again. An application can have comfortable numerical margins while still raising a timing or status question.

Beyond the day count

The Home Office can exercise discretion over excess absences, and its caseworker guidance says how. Where the overall total is no more than 30 days over the limit, caseworkers normally overlook the excess if everything else is in order: up to 480 days on the standard route and 300 on the spouse route. In the final 12 months, up to 100 days is normally overlooked on the spouse route, and on the standard route when the future-plans requirement is met.

Larger excesses, up to 900 days on the standard route and 540 on the spouse route, need an established home, work, family and finances in the UK, plus a reason such as Crown service or compelling work or family circumstances. Beyond that, discretion is unlikely. None of this is an automatic allowance: an application that relies on it is assessed case by case, with evidence.

Lawful residence, immigration status, language and knowledge requirements, good character and the other conditions of the route remain separate. Citizenship absence limits are also distinct from ILR continuous residence, visitor stay rules and the Statutory Residence Test for tax. One travel record can support those different assessments without making their rules interchangeable.

FAQ

Do departure and return days count as absences?

No. Naturalisation counts whole days outside the relevant territory. Leaving on one day and returning the next produces no whole absence days.

Are the Channel Islands and Isle of Man included?

Yes. For these nationality rules, presence in the Channel Islands and Isle of Man counts with presence in the UK. They are Crown Dependencies, not part of the UK in the usual geographical sense.

Can I use the spouse route if my marriage has ended?

No. You must be married to, or the civil partner of, a British citizen on the date you apply. After a divorce, a dissolution or your partner's death, the standard five-year route applies.

Does staying within both limits make me eligible for citizenship?

It satisfies the two ordinary numerical absence limits only. First-day presence, immigration status and the other requirements still matter, and naturalisation remains discretionary.
