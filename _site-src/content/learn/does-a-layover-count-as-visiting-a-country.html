Does a Layover Count as Visiting a Country?

It is two questions. Whether a layover counts for visas and day limits has a legal answer, and it turns on border control. Whether it counts on your own travel list is your call.

Last verified: September 2026

In short: for immigration and day counts, a layover counts only if you pass through border control. An airside connection in a Schengen airport is not an entry and adds no days to 90/180. Airside transit in the UK needs no ETA. The US has no airside transit: you clear border control at the first US airport, although under 24 hours in transit between two foreign places is left out of the US tax day count. For your personal travel list there is no official rule, so pick a standard and apply it every time.

The immigration question: airside or landside

For visas, stay limits and tax day counts, what matters is not whether your plane landed but whether you entered the country. The UK government describes the two kinds of transit plainly: airside, where you do not pass through border control before your connecting flight, and landside, where you pass through border control and leave again within a short time, usually 24 hours.

  • Schengen area. Staying in an airport's international transit area is not entering the Schengen area. Most travelers need no visa for it, but nationals of some countries need an airport transit visa even airside. Changing terminals, or a final destination inside the Schengen area, can mean passing border control at the stopover. Not every Schengen airport has an international transit area.
  • United Kingdom. Airside transit passengers do not need an ETA, and your airline can tell you whether your connection is airside. Some nationalities need a Direct Airside Transit visa. If you go through border control, normal ETA or visa rules apply, and a Visitor in Transit visa covers those who need one and leave within 48 hours.
  • United States. There is no airside option for immigration. Travelers clear border control at the first US airport, or at a preclearance airport abroad, even when connecting onward. Visa Waiver Program travelers need an approved ESTA or a visa to transit the US.

Does a layover add days to a stay limit or tax count?

An airside connection adds nothing to a Schengen count, because there is no entry date to start a stay. Once you pass border control, the layover is an ordinary short stay, and most rules count it like any other day.

Two routes through Frankfurt. You fly New York to Rome, changing planes in Frankfurt on 10 June. Rome is inside the Schengen area, so you pass Schengen border control in Frankfurt, and 10 June is your first Schengen day. Fly New York to Istanbul through Frankfurt instead and stay in the international transit area, and the connection adds no Schengen days.

A few rules have their own transit exceptions, and they are narrow:

  • UK Statutory Residence Test. A transit arrival day does not count if you arrive as a passenger, leave the next day as a passenger, and do nothing substantially unrelated to the journey in between. An airport hotel and breakfast is fine; meeting friends is not.
  • US Substantial Presence Test. Days in the US for less than 24 hours while in transit between two places outside the US are not counted, even though you passed border control.

The travel-list question

For your own list of visited countries, no authority decides. Some travelers count only places where they left the airport. Others count any formal entry. Some want real time on the ground beyond the transport chain. These are personal standards, not legal ones. Which places count as countries in the first place is a separate choice, covered in how many countries there are, and why lists differ.

Three common standards

  • Strict: it counts only if you left the airport or had a meaningful visit on the ground.
  • Moderate: it counts if you formally entered the country through border control, even briefly.
  • Lenient: landing there is enough.

None of these is wrong. What matters is applying one rule consistently instead of deciding case by case, and keeping your personal list separate from the legal count. An airside layover that goes on a lenient travel list still adds no days to a visa limit.

How to record a layover so it does not distort your counts

  1. Note whether you passed through border control. An entry stamp, an electronic entry record or a landside hotel night means you entered.
  2. Record an airside connection as transit, separate from your stays, with zero days. Keep it, since it can still explain a route later.
  3. Record a landside layover as a short stay with its real dates. It can count toward limits and tax tests, subject to the transit exceptions above.
  4. Keep your travel-list standard separate from the legal record, so a country can be on your list without inflating a day count, or the reverse.

In a spreadsheet, a transit column that you exclude from every day total does the job. Beyond the day count

Whether a connection is airside depends on the airport, the terminal and your ticket, and your airline is the one to confirm it. Transit visa requirements depend on your nationality and change over time, so check the official rules for each airport on your route before you fly, not after.

FAQ

Does an airside layover count toward the Schengen 90 days?

No. Staying in the international transit area of a Schengen airport is not an entry into the Schengen area, so it adds no days. If you pass border control during the stopover, that day counts as a full day of stay.

Do I need a UK ETA for a layover?

Not if you stay airside and do not pass through UK border control, as of September 2026. Some nationalities need a Direct Airside Transit visa instead. If you go through border control, the normal ETA or visa rules apply.

Does a US layover count as a day for US tax?

Usually not. You pass US border control even when connecting, but the Substantial Presence Test does not count days when you are in the US for less than 24 hours in transit between two places outside the US.
