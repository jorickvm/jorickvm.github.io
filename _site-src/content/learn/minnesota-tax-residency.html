Minnesota's 183-Day Statutory Residency Rule

Minnesota’s 183-day rule combines presence with an abode. The statute uses more than half the tax year, and the rules contain an important transit exception.

Last verified: August 2026

In short: Minnesota’s published guidance describes the 183-day rule: presence of at least 183 days together with an abode can establish residence despite a domicile elsewhere. The statute’s precise test is more than half the tax year plus an abode. Partial days generally count, but qualifying transit between outside points is excluded.

The rule

Minnesota has two separate ways to be a resident: by domicile (Minnesota is your true, permanent home) or by the 183-day rule. The 183-day rule is the statutory-residence test, and it has two conditions that must both be met:

  • An abode in Minnesota. You or your spouse rent, own, maintain, or occupy an abode in the state. An abode is a residence suitable for year-round use and equipped with its own cooking and bathing facilities. A cabin or cottage is excluded when it is both unsuitable for year-round use and used only for vacations. Calling a year-round home a vacation cabin does not by itself exclude it.
  • More than half the tax year. That is 183 days in a full 365-day year, consistent with the Department’s 183-day summary. For a leap or shortened tax year, do not assume that the statute’s more-than-half wording is a fixed 183-day threshold.

Meeting the applicable presence and abode conditions can establish residence even if you are domiciled elsewhere, subject to the statutory exceptions. Filing duties and the calculation of Minnesota-taxable income remain separate questions.

How to count it

  1. Confirm you or your spouse maintained an abode in Minnesota suitable for year-round use.
  2. Count days spent wholly or partly in Minnesota. Exclude transit between two points outside Minnesota when the Minnesota transit lasts less than 24 hours. The rule’s example excludes both dates of a qualifying 7 p.m. arrival and 1 p.m. departure the next day.
  3. Add the counted days across the tax year. They do not need to be consecutive, and the abode does not have to be where you sleep on every counted day.
  4. Compare the total with the applicable presence threshold and confirm the abode condition and statutory exceptions. If the abode applies for only part of the year, the Department treats that period as part-year residence when the presence test is met.

Example. You are domiciled in Florida but keep a lakeside house in Minnesota, wired for year-round use, all year. Across the year you are physically in Minnesota on 190 days, many of them just for part of the day.

Assume none of those 190 days qualifies for the transit exception and no military or reciprocity exclusion applies. With a qualifying abode all year, 190 counted days exceeds half of a full 365- or 366-day tax year, so both conditions are met despite your Florida domicile.

Beyond the day count

The day test does not replace domicile, your true, fixed home. The statutory day route excludes qualifying military members or spouses and individuals covered by Minnesota’s reciprocity rules. Eligibility and the treatment of income beyond covered wages need separate review. Keep records of days outside Minnesota and the purpose and timing of transit; a trip described as transit is not enough without meeting the rule’s conditions.

FAQ

How many days can you spend in Minnesota without becoming a statutory resident?

In a full 365-day tax year, 183 counted days exceeds half the year; the abode condition and exceptions must also be considered. The statute uses more than half the tax year, so the year’s actual length matters. Domicile can establish residence separately.

What counts as a day in Minnesota?

Any part of a day generally counts, even when you do not sleep at the abode. Qualifying transit between two outside points lasting less than 24 hours in Minnesota is excluded.

Do you need a home in Minnesota to be caught by the rule?

For the 183-day rule, yes. Without an abode suitable for year-round use there is no statutory residence on days alone, though separate domicile rules can still make you a resident.
