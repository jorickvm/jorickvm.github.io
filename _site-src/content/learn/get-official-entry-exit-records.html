How to Get Your Official Travel History: Entry and Exit Records by Country

Where to request the border authority's own record of your arrivals and departures in the US, Canada, Australia, the UK, the Schengen area and New Zealand, what each record shows and misses, and how long it takes.

Last verified: September 2026

In short: each country only records its own border, so you request each record separately. Visitors to the US can download 10 years of I-94 history online in minutes. US citizens and green card holders file a FOIA request with CBP, online only since 22 January 2026. Canada's Travel History Report is free and takes up to 30 days. Australia releases movement records from 1981. For the Schengen area, the Entry/Exit System has recorded non-EU travelers' crossings since 12 October 2025, and you can ask for that data. The UK has no dedicated travel-history request.

United States: I-94 history for visitors

If you are not a US citizen or green card holder, the I-94 website shows your arrival and departure history for the past 10 years, up to 100 records, including travel on passports that have since expired. You search with your passport details and your name as it appears in the machine-readable zone of the passport. Separately, the site shows your most recent I-94 admission record.

  • What it misses. CBP says most land border arrivals and departures, closed-loop cruises and land departures into Mexico may not appear, and neither do changes of status made through USCIS.
  • Its legal weight. CBP describes the history as a helpful tool, not an official record for legal purposes. Save a copy, but treat it as a strong anchor rather than the final word.
  • If it is wrong. For an error in your travel history, request redress through the CBP Information Center or DHS TRIP (one process, not both), with supporting evidence. An error in the I-94 admission itself goes to a CBP Deferred Inspection office, within 30 days of admission.

United States: FOIA for citizens and green card holders

US citizens and lawful permanent residents cannot use the I-94 site. They request their records from CBP under the Freedom of Information Act, and so can anyone else who wants a fuller record. CBP's FOIA page says that since 22 January 2026 it no longer accepts requests by mail, fax or email: file through the SecureRelease portal or FOIA.gov. (One older CBP page still lists a postal address; the January 2026 notice is the current instruction.)

  • What to ask for. Your own records of international travel to and from the US. CBP's travel records go back to 1982.
  • Identity. Full name, address, date of birth and a signed certification of identity, or a statement under penalty of perjury.
  • Cost. There is no fee to file. Individuals can be charged for copies beyond 100 pages and searches beyond two hours.

Canada: Travel History Report

The CBSA issues a Travel History Report as a free request under the Privacy Act, made online through the government's Access to Information and Privacy portal. Anyone can ask, in or outside Canada. Processing can take up to 30 days.

  • Entries show the date and port of entry, with your name, date of birth, citizenship and document numbers.
  • Exits are included only if you ask. Say whether you want entries, exits or both. Exit data exists for land crossings since 30 June 2013 for foreign nationals other than US citizens, for all travelers at land borders since 11 July 2019, and for commercial flights since 25 June 2020.
  • Applying for citizenship or a PR card? The CBSA says not to request the report yourself for those applications: IRCC can obtain the information itself, and a separate request can significantly delay the application.

Australia: international movement records

The Department of Home Affairs has an online Request for International Movement Records form. The record lists your arrival and departure dates from 1981 onward, the number of movements, the Australian port, flight or vessel details, passport numbers, and the visa you held at the time. One request covers every passport you have held.

It does not show which countries you traveled to or from. An Australian record proves when you left and returned, not where you were in between. Records before 1981 are held by the National Archives.

Schengen area: Entry/Exit System data

The Entry/Exit System (EES) records the date and place of each entry and exit for non-EU travelers on short stays, and replaces manual passport stamping. It started on 12 October 2025 and has been fully operational at all Schengen external borders since 10 April 2026.

  • Requesting your data. Contact a data controller or data protection officer in any country that uses the EES, preferably one you traveled to. You can ask to access, correct or delete your data.
  • How long it exists. Entry and exit records are kept for three years, or five years if no exit was recorded. Request what you need before it ages out.
  • The stay checker. The EU's "check how long you can stay" service reads your EES record from your passport details and tells you whether a planned stay is within the limit and how many days you have left. It does not list your past crossings, and it does not count Schengen stays that began before 10 April 2026. See EES, ETIAS and the 90/180 rule.

Before October 2025, passport stamps are the Schengen record, and travel between Schengen countries was never recorded at all.

United Kingdom: a subject access request

The UK has no travel-history report. The closest route is a free subject access request to the Home Office Subject Access Request Unit, which handles information held by UK Visas and Immigration, immigration enforcement and Border Force. The basic request covers the digital information recorded about you since 2000. The Home Office replies within one month of receiving your supporting documents, and can extend that for complex requests.

Apply with the online form from a UK address, or by email from abroad, with photo ID and a signed, recent letter of authority. The guidance does not mention travel movements or exit records, so do not count on receiving a list of your departures. For UK tax residence, your own records of days and midnights carry the case; see how to prove you were not in a country.

New Zealand: travel movements

New Zealand Customs releases your travel movements under the Privacy Act 2020. Email form NZCS 150 to Customs with a copy of your passport photo page or driver licence. The record shows the dates you arrived in and left New Zealand, from the early 1990s onward, and Customs asks you to allow up to 20 working days.

Reconciling an official record with your own log

An official record is one border's view. Lay it beside your own chronology, trip by trip, and expect three kinds of mismatch:

  1. A crossing is missing. Land borders are the usual gap. Look for the other side of the same border: a country that records entries often fills a gap in the record of the country you left.
  2. A date is off by one day. Check for overnight flights, arrivals after midnight and time zones before assuming either record is wrong. The border record shows the local date of the crossing.
  3. The record shows travel that did not happen. Use the correction route: CBP redress or DHS TRIP for I-94 history, a correction request to an EES data controller for Schengen. Send evidence, and keep a copy of what you sent.

Filling a US land gap with a Canadian record. Your I-94 history shows an arrival at JFK on 2 May 2024 and the next arrival on 20 August, with no departure in between, because you drove to Canada on 30 June. Canada's Travel History Report shows your entry at a land port on 30 June. That Canadian entry is the evidence of your US departure, and it limits your US stay to 2 May – 30 June: 60 days if both the arrival and departure day count, as they do for the substantial presence test.

If your own log disagrees with an official record and you have no better evidence, adopt the official date and note the change. If you do have better evidence, such as a boarding pass for a different day, keep your date, note the conflict, and be ready to explain it.

Request early

Canada allows up to 30 days, New Zealand up to 20 working days, the UK one month after it has your documents, and CBP publishes no FOIA timeline. If an application, an audit or a tax return depends on these records, request them as soon as you know you need them, and request every country involved at the same time. EES records are kept for three years from each crossing, so older crossings will not stay available indefinitely.

Beyond the printout

An official record anchors dates, but it rarely tells the whole story: it covers one border, misses some crossings and says nothing about where you were in between. FAQ

How do I get my US travel history as a US citizen?

File a Freedom of Information Act request with CBP through the SecureRelease portal or FOIA.gov, asking for your records of international travel to and from the United States. Since 22 January 2026, CBP no longer accepts requests by mail, fax or email. The I-94 website does not cover US citizens or green card holders.

Can I get a list of my Schengen entries and exits?

For crossings recorded in the Entry/Exit System, from 12 October 2025, you can request your data from a data controller in a Schengen country you traveled to. Earlier trips exist only as passport stamps, and travel between Schengen countries is not recorded.

Does an official record show which countries I visited?

Usually not. Each country records its own border. Australia's record, for example, shows when you left and returned but not where you went, so your own records have to fill in the rest.

Can I get my UK travel history from the Home Office?

There is no dedicated UK travel-history report. You can make a free subject access request for the immigration and Border Force information the Home Office holds about you, but the guidance does not promise a list of your entries and exits.
