The Czech Republic's 183-Day Tax Residency Rule

At least 183 days in a calendar year is one Czech tax-residence route. Check the separate home test, the solely-study or treatment exception, and any applicable treaty.

Last verified: 12 November 2026

In short: you become a Czech tax resident if you spend at least 183 days (183 or more) in the Czech Republic during a single calendar year, with the domestic day-count status applying from 1 January. Every commenced day counts, including arrival and departure. A person staying solely for study or medical treatment is treated as non-resident under a specific exception. Outside that exception, an available permanent home with an intention to live there is a separate residence route; treaty treatment needs separate attention.

The rule

The Czech Republic treats you as a tax resident by habitual abode if you are present there for at least 183 days in a single calendar year, whether continuously or across several separate stays. A few points decide most real cases:

  • 183 or more is enough. The test is at least 183 days, not more than 183. Unlike rules elsewhere that need you to exceed 183 days, in the Czech Republic exactly 183 days already makes you resident.
  • The window is the calendar year. The 183 days are counted from 1 January to 31 December. Meeting this day-count criterion establishes domestic residence for the calendar year from 1 January. An applicable tax treaty can alter the position for part of the year.
  • Every commenced day counts. Each started day of stay counts, so any part of a day spent in the Czech Republic counts as a full day. Both your arrival day and your departure day count.
  • Study or treatment is a status exception. A person staying solely for study or medical treatment is treated as non-resident even if the usual presence or home criteria are met. Combining study with work or business can take the stay outside this exception; it is not a rule for subtracting individual study days from a mixed stay.

How to count it

  1. List every Czech Republic trip with arrival and departure dates.
  2. Count each commenced day, including both arrival and departure days. Assess the solely-study or treatment exception separately.
  3. Total the days that fall inside a single calendar year, from 1 January to 31 December.
  4. If the total reaches 183 days or more, the domestic day-count criterion is met. Check the status exception and any applicable treaty before deciding your final residence position.

Example. 90 days in the Czech Republic in spring, then 93 more days across two shorter autumn stays, all in the same calendar year.

No single stay is long, but the days total exactly 183 in that one calendar year. Assuming the solely-study or treatment exception does not apply, the domestic day-count criterion makes you resident for the year from 1 January. A treaty may change the final result.

Beyond the day count

The 183-day count is one route in, not the only one. The Czech Republic can also treat you as resident if you have a permanent home or place of abode there (a bydliště) under circumstances indicating an intention to reside there permanently. Either test is enough on its own, so you can be resident on the permanent-home basis even below 183 days. Note that simply holding a long-term visa or residence permit does not by itself create tax residency – it is your actual presence and living situation that count. If another country also treats you as resident and an applicable double-tax treaty exists, its residence provisions may change the result, including for part of the year.

FAQ

How many days can you stay in the Czech Republic without becoming a tax resident?

Under the day-count criterion, 182 days is below the threshold and 183 meets it. A permanent home can establish residence separately, and the solely-study or treatment exception and treaty position still need review.

Does exactly 183 days count?

Yes. The Czech test is 183 or more, so exactly 183 days is enough, unlike rules that require more than 183 days.

Do study or medical stays count?

A person staying solely for study or medical treatment is treated as non-resident under a specific exception. Study combined with work or business can fall outside it; do not simply subtract study days from a mixed stay.
