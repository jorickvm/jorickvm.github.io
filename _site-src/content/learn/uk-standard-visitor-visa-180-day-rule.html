UK Standard Visitor: How Length of Stay Is Assessed

A practical explainer of what the UK visitor "180-day rule" shorthand usually means, where it misleads, and why travel pattern matters alongside length of stay.

Last verified: June 2026

In short: a Standard Visitor can usually stay for up to 6 months per visit, which is where the "180-day rule" shorthand comes from. It is not a Schengen-style rolling formula and not a published 180-days-per-year entitlement. Alongside length of stay, the UK also asks whether you are genuinely visiting rather than effectively living in the UK through repeated trips.

What the shorthand gets right, and what it misses

GOV.UK and Appendix V both frame a Standard Visitor stay as usually up to 6 months, and in conversation people translate that into "180 days." The number is roughly right. Treating it as the whole test is not, because the visitor route runs on more than one idea at once:

  • Visit length: a Standard Visitor can usually stay for up to 6 months.
  • Genuine visitor status: you must intend to leave at the end of the visit and not use frequent or successive visits to make the UK your main home.
  • Purpose and credibility: the visit must fit the permitted activities and the overall circumstances.

A pure day count does not settle the question by itself. Time in the UK matters, and so does the pattern that time creates.

How pattern is actually assessed

Current UK caseworker guidance makes the pattern issue explicit. Decision makers are told to consider factors including:

  • the number of visits made over the last 12 months
  • how long each stay lasted
  • how much time elapsed between visits
  • whether the overall pattern suggests the person is spending more time in the UK than in their home country
  • whether return trips seem to be used mainly to seek re-entry

None of that creates a formula like "180 days in 12 months equals refusal." It means the pattern over time can serve as evidence of whether someone is really visiting temporarily. The practical consequence is that two travellers with the same total number of UK days can be assessed differently: one long planned trip reads as a visit, while the same days spread across many short returns with brief absences between them can read as living in the UK part-time.

Four assumptions that cause trouble

  • "There is a fixed 180-day-in-12-months test." There is not. The UK does not work like Schengen.
  • "Every departure resets a fresh full stay." The next entry is still judged in the context of your wider visitor pattern.
  • "A long-term visit visa answers the residence question." Long-term visit visas are still for genuine visits, not de facto residence.
  • "Only one long trip counts." Repeated shorter trips can matter just as much if the cumulative pattern looks residential.

The safest working model is that up to 6 months per visit is not a free-standing entitlement to spend half of every year in the UK. Do not treat 6 months as a target, and do not assume a short absence between long visits resolves the wider pattern question.

Where to check, and where to stop

  • For the route itself, use GOV.UK's Standard Visitor overview.
  • For the formal rule text, use Appendix V: Visitor.
  • For how travel pattern is assessed in practice, use the current visit caseworker guidance.

If you are travelling on an ETA rather than a visa, note that the ETA is only travel permission. It does not displace the Standard Visitor rules, so the same 6-month framing, genuine-visitor requirement, and pattern assessment still apply to the stay itself.
